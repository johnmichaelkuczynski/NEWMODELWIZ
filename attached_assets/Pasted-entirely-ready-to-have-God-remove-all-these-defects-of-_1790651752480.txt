Section 2 must now address AA's failures, considering whether the superego-projection model accounts for them. The model predicts that AA will fail when an alcoholic cannot maintain the projection needed for surrender or when his superego contains pathological elements that render alignment with it detrimental. For instance, if an individual's superego is overly punitive or rooted in unrealistic standards, aligning conduct with such a superego could exacerbate feelings of guilt and inadequacy, potentially leading to relapse rather than recovery. Additionally, AA's requirement for ongoing belief in the higher power, while crucial for maintaining superego projection, becomes a source of vulnerability. If an individual loses faith in this externalized superego, the psychological structure sustaining sobriety collapses, leading to a high relapse risk (Tonigan et al., 2017). This could explain why some alcoholics, despite initially succeeding in AA, eventually falter: they cannot sustain the necessary belief in their projected superego. The model thus accounts for AA's failures by identifying the stability of the superego projection as a critical variable. If the projection is unstable or if the superego is itself problematic, the likelihood of sustained recovery diminishes, aligning with observed relapse patterns.

The potential for failure inherent in AA's model extends beyond just the individual's capacity to sustain belief. The psychological architecture of the superego itself plays a crucial role in determining the success or failure of AA's methodology. If the superego is marked by pathological traits, such as extreme self-criticism or unrealistic expectations, aligning one's actions with such a superego can be psychologically damaging. This misalignment can increase the individual's internal conflict rather than resolve it, leading to a cycle of guilt, self-reproach, and eventual relapse.

Moreover, the model predicts that AA's effectiveness hinges on the individual's ability to project their superego consistently. If the projection becomes unstable—due to external stressors, changes in life circumstances, or diminished psychological resilience—the person may struggle to maintain sobriety. This instability can lead to a reversion to old behaviors, as the superego projection no longer provides the necessary psychological support to resist the urge to drink.

The requirement for continuous engagement with AA practices, such as meetings and sponsorship, serves as a mechanism to reinforce and stabilize the superego projection. However, this dependency also means that any disruption in these practices could jeopardize the individual's sobriety. For instance, if a person becomes isolated from their support network or if their belief in the effectiveness of the program wanes, the psychological framework supporting their sobriety can collapse.

Additionally, the model's reliance on a projected superego suggests that individuals with a less developed or fragmented superego may find it particularly challenging to achieve long-term recovery through AA. Such individuals might lack the internal resources necessary to project a coherent and supportive higher power, making the process of surrender less effective.

In summary, the superego-projection model provides a framework for understanding both the successes and failures of AA. It highlights the necessity of a stable and healthy superego for sustaining recovery and underscores the importance of maintaining the projection of this superego onto a higher power. When this projection is unstable or the superego is inherently problematic, the likelihood of relapse increases, explaining the variability in outcomes among AA participants. This understanding not only accounts for the documented patterns of success and failure in AA but also suggests areas for further research and potential interventions to support those for whom the traditional AA model may be insufficient.

The recognition that some individuals may possess a fragmented or underdeveloped superego challenges the assumption that AA's methodology can universally apply to all severe alcoholics. For these individuals, the projection onto a higher power might lack the cohesive moral and ethical guidelines necessary for effective behavior modification. Consequently, their attempts at surrender may fail to achieve the intended realignment with moral values, leaving them susceptible to relapse. This insight suggests that AA's model might benefit from complementary approaches tailored to enhance superego development or provide alternative frameworks of moral guidance. Such interventions could include individual therapy aimed at strengthening the superego or integrating elements from other therapeutic models that address specific psychological deficits hindering the projection process. By acknowledging and addressing these limitations, AA could improve its success rate and better accommodate the diverse psychological profiles of its participants. This expanded understanding underscores the importance of considering individual differences in psychological structure when evaluating the effectiveness of AA's superego-projection mechanism.
