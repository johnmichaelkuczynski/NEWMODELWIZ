Section 1:

A directed obligation is a duty owed to a specific person such that only that person has standing to demand performance, waive the duty, or claim remedy for breach, and the wrongness of breach consists in wronging that particular person rather than merely doing something impermissible. This contrasts with a general obligation, where the duty holder must perform the act but no particular individual holds special authority over the duty's performance or release. The obligation not to lie is general in most contexts: I must tell the truth, but no specific person can release me from this duty when I am about to lie to a third party. If I promise you to return your book tomorrow, however, you alone can tell me "never mind, keep it," and thereby eliminate my duty. A stranger who overhears the promise cannot release me, nor can they demand I return the book with the same authority you possess.

Promises exhibit the defining structure of directed obligations. When I promise you to meet at noon, I create a duty that runs specifically to you. You have standing to demand that I show up, to waive the requirement if your plans change, and to claim some remedial response—an apology, an explanation, perhaps compensation—if I fail to appear. Your mother, who wants us to meet so we can plan her surprise party, lacks this standing. She may hope I arrive and be disappointed if I do not, but she cannot release me from the promise, and my failure does not wrong her in the way it wrongs you. The asymmetry is not merely social or conventional politeness; it reflects the structure of the obligation itself.

The promisee's power to release the promisor demonstrates that the obligation runs specifically to the promisee. Suppose I promise you to donate 500 to charity by Friday. On Thursday you tell me, "I release you from that promise; spend the money however you like." The duty vanishes. I may still have reasons to donate—I might think it would be good to help others, or I might have independently decided to give—but I no longer have a promissory obligation to do so. If the obligation were general—a duty to donate that I happened to communicate to you—your release would be ineffective. A general duty to donate500 does not disappear because one person says I need not fulfill it. The fact that your statement eliminates my duty shows the duty was owed to you.

Third parties lack this power. If your colleague, who knows about my promise and approves of charitable giving, tells me "I release you from your promise to donate," her statement has no normative force. She can express an opinion, but she cannot waive my duty. The difference is not that you made the promise while she did not; the difference is that the obligation runs to you. If promissory obligations were general, there would be no principled reason why your release succeeds while hers fails. Both of you are members of the moral community, both can observe my promise, both can have preferences about my action. The asymmetry in release power reveals an asymmetry in the obligation's structure.

The same asymmetry appears in standing to demand performance. You can call me at 11:55 and say, "You promised to meet me at noon; are you coming?" This demand has normative force. I owe you an answer, and if I am not coming, I owe you an explanation or apology. Your colleague, who knows about the promise and thinks it important that I keep my word, cannot make the same demand with the same authority. She can ask where I am going or express hope that I keep promises generally, but she cannot demand that I meet you in the way you can. If she calls and says, "You promised to meet at noon; you must show up," I can correctly respond, "That's between me and the person I promised." I cannot say this to you.

The promisee's standing to claim remedy after breach further demonstrates directedness. If I break my promise to meet you, you have authority to demand an apology, an explanation, or compensation for costs you incurred in reliance. Your colleague, who is disappointed that I broke my word, lacks this authority. She can criticize me for promise-breaking in general, but she cannot claim that I owe her an apology or compensation. The wrongness of my breach is not merely that I did something impermissible—failed to keep my word, disrupted social coordination, or acted viciously. The wrongness is that I wronged you. This is why you, and not others, have standing to claim remedy.

Consider the contrast with a genuine general obligation. I have a duty not to steal. If I steal your car, I wrong you specifically, but the duty not to steal is not directed to you in the relevant sense. Before I stole your car, you had no power to release me from the duty not to steal it. You could consent to my taking it, which would make the taking not theft, but you could not waive a general duty that forbids stealing from everyone. The duty not to steal is general; it applies to all potential victims. When I promise you to return your book, the structure is different. The duty to return the book exists only because I promised you, and you have the power to eliminate it.
